## 1. Rationale and Research Basis

### 1.1 Why a Level 4, and who it is for

Level 3 graduates can keep a conversation going with follow-up questions and reasons, tell what happened with the past continuous, say how life used to be, offer help with *will*, and say what will happen *if…*. What they usually **can't** do yet is talk about **experiences that connect the past and the present** (*I've worked here since 2022. Have you ever…?*), talk about **imaginary situations** (*If I had more time, I'd…*), say what **might** happen, and **give and defend an opinion** politely. Those are the skills adults need for a health history form, a talk with a supervisor, a community meeting, a news report, or a suspicious phone call. Level 4 teaches them.

**Target learner:** an adult who finished Level 3 (or places at that level) and can hold a conversation on familiar topics with some help. About 95% speak Spanish; the rest speak a range of other languages. Most read and write in their first language. Some are repeating learners who want more speaking practice.

### 1.2 Level estimates (our estimate, not an official alignment)

| Framework | Level 3 (existing) | Level 4 (this course) | Level 5 (planned) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **NRS** Educational Functioning Levels for ESL (U.S. Department of Education, adult education reporting) | High Beginning → **Low Intermediate ESL** | Starts at **Low Intermediate ESL**; aims to finish at **High Intermediate ESL** | High Intermediate → **Advanced ESL** |
| **CEFR** (Council of Europe; "plus" levels from the 2020 *Companion Volume*) | A2 → A2+ | **A2+ → B1** | B1 → B1+ (moving toward B2) |
| **CASAS** | Same life-skill content areas (see 1.3). CASAS tests can report learners on the NRS levels. We **do not** claim any score alignment | Same | Same |

What these levels describe, in plain words (paraphrased):

- **NRS Low Intermediate ESL:** the learner understands familiar phrases and some new ones spoken slowly with repetition, can ask and answer questions, and can take part in some routine social conversations, though with some difficulty.
- **NRS High Intermediate ESL:** the learner understands short new phrases on familiar topics, can take part in limited conversations, and has inconsistent control of more complex grammar.
- **CEFR A2+:** the stronger half of A2: the learner takes a more active part in conversation (with some help) and handles a wider range of everyday situations.
- **CEFR B1:** the learner understands the main points of clear speech on familiar matters (work, school, free time), deals with most everyday situations, connects sentences to describe experiences, events, hopes, and plans, and gives short reasons and explanations.

**The Level 4 exit goal is NRS High Intermediate and CEFR B1, applied to work, family, health, and community situations.** The can-do lists in Section 2 are written to match.

> **Note on test scores:** CASAS publishes score ranges for each NRS level, and the ranges differ by test series and year. Newer NRS descriptors are aligned with the federal English Language Proficiency Standards for adult education. If the program needs scores for reporting, use the current official tables, not this document.

### 1.4 Teaching principles (and where they come from)

| Principle | What it means in Level 4 | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Three sentences, not one** | Every Talk Time and every exit ticket asks for an answer of about three connected sentences: the answer, a reason, and a detail or example. | CEFR B1 descriptors for connecting sentences and giving short reasons and explanations |
| **Grammar through real tasks** | Each grammar point is tied to one real job: present perfect → a health history form; *Would you mind…?* → asking a neighbor for a favor; *might* → understanding a news report; *who / that* → spotting a scam. | Task-based language teaching (for example, Rod Ellis, *Task-Based Language Learning and Teaching*, 2003) |
| **Explicit, easy-to-explain grammar** | Every box has a one-line "rule of thumb" and, for the present perfect, a timeline the teacher can draw. Volunteers never need grammar terms beyond the box. | Focus on form within meaning-focused tasks |
| **Pictures with every word** | Every vocabulary word has a picture card, as in Levels 1–3. | Dual coding (Allan Paivio) |
| **Many meetings with each word** | Each word appears in New Words, the listening, the grammar box, Talk Time, Read & Write, the homework, and later lessons. Learners keep their own word list. | Paul Nation, *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language* |
| **Listen for the main idea, then details** | The audio is longer, at natural speed with some reductions (*gonna, wanna*), so the first listen is for gist and the second for details. | Common listening-instruction practice (pre-, while-, and post-listening stages) |
| **Lots of pair and group talk** | Every lesson has at least three pair or group tasks, and Talk Time is 3 minutes longer than in Level 3. Target: learners talk for more than half of the class time. | Same principle as Levels 1–3 |

## 2. Entry and Exit Skills

### 2.1 Entry (who should start in Level 4)

A learner belongs in Level 4 if, in a short placement chat, they **can** do most of these:

- tell a short story in the past with *when* or *while* (*I was waiting for the bus when it started to rain*)
- say how life used to be (*I used to live on a farm*)
- say what they will do *if* something happens, with a reason (*If it rains, I'll take the bus, because I don't have an umbrella*)

…but **cannot yet** comfortably do most of these:

- answer *Have you ever…?* and *How long have you lived here?* correctly (*I've lived here for three years*, not *I live here since three years*)
- talk about an imaginary situation (*If I had more time, I would…*)
- disagree politely (*I see your point, but…*)

Learners who can't yet do the first list probably belong in Level 3. Learners who can do both lists probably belong in Level 5.

### 2.2 Exit (what learners can do at the end = what Level 5 expects)

**Talking and listening:** learners can…

- talk about life experiences (*Have you ever…?*) and how long (*for / since*)
- describe their health history and an ongoing symptom (*I've had this rash since Monday*)
- say what has and hasn't been done yet at work (*I've already restocked the shelves. I haven't cleaned the cooler yet*)
- ask a neighbor or coworker for a favor, and answer one politely (*Would you mind…? No, not at all*)
- talk about imaginary situations (*If I had…, I would…*)
- give, ask for, and politely disagree with an opinion
- tell about life events in order (*before, after, when*)
- talk about cultural expectations such as being on time and workplace dress
- understand the main points of a short local news report
- recognize a scam and say what to do
- talk about possibility (*It might close early*)
- ask for time off, plan a trip, and leave a detailed voicemail
- give a 2-minute talk about their story and next step

**Reading and writing:** learners can…

- read texts of 150–250 words (notices, emails, an employee handbook page, a utility bill and flyer, a short news article, an advice column) and find the main idea and key details
- write a short email of 5–8 sentences (an update, a favor, a time-off request, a warning to a friend)
- write a short opinion paragraph (5–7 sentences) with a topic sentence, two reasons, and a closing sentence

**Grammar they control (in practiced situations):** present perfect (*ever / never, for / since, already / yet / just*) and present perfect vs. simple past; suggestions (*could, why don't you, it's a good idea to*); *Would you mind…?*; second conditional; *too / enough, too much / too many*; *must / mustn't / don't have to*; *might / may / could* for possibility; *Do you know where…?*; agreeing and disagreeing; *as … as*; separable phrasal verbs; infinitive of purpose; verb + infinitive vs. verb + *-ing*; defining relative clauses with *who / that*; time clauses with *before / after / when*; *could / was able to* for past ability.

**Deliberately NOT in Level 4 (Level 5 teaches these):** present perfect continuous (*How long have you been working…?*; Level 4 uses *How long have you worked…?*), past perfect, *wish*, third conditional, passive voice, reported speech, embedded questions with *if / whether* (Level 4 uses only *Do you know where / when / what time / why…?*), tag questions, modals of deduction (*It must be…*), relative clauses with *which / where* and object relative clauses as a taught point, and *unless / as long as*.

## 3. Handoff from Level 3 and to Level 5

### 3.1 What Level 3 teaches and Level 4 assumes

Level 4 Lesson 1 opens with a **brief** past-tense warm-up, then moves straight to the present perfect. Everything below is **not re-taught**; it appears only in the 6:00 Opening Talk, in a homework item, or as support for a new point.

| Level 4 assumes learners know… | Level 3 teaches it in | Where Level 4 uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Follow-up questions; *because / so* | Lesson 1 | Every Talk Time and exit ticket; Lesson 9 (opinions); Lesson 13 (with *before / after / when*) |
| *look like / be like*; object pronouns | Lesson 2 | Lesson 3 (*I've cleaned it*); Lesson 10 (*turn it off*); Lesson 12 (*a man who…*) |
| *used to* | Lesson 3 | Lesson 2 (*I used to live…, but I've lived here since…*); Lesson 13 |
| Past continuous + *when / while* | Lesson 4 | Lesson 8 (news); Lesson 13 (longer life stories) |
| *will* (offers, decisions, predictions); *will* vs. *going to* | Lessons 5, 6, 13 | Lesson 5 (*Sure, I'll do it*); Lesson 8 (*will* vs. *might*); Lesson 11 (plans) |
| First conditional | Lesson 6 | Lesson 6 contrasts it with the second conditional; Lesson 13 (*When I finish…, I'll…*) |
| *I'd like to* + verb; *Could I / May I…?* | Lesson 7 | Lesson 5 (*Would you mind…?*); Lesson 11 (*I'd like to request…*) |
| Possessive pronouns; *Whose…?* | Lesson 8 | Lesson 5 (borrowing: *Could I borrow yours?*), review only |
| *good at / interested in / enjoy / don't mind* + *-ing* | Lesson 9 | Lesson 11 (verb + *-ing* vs. verb + infinitive) |
| Sequence words + imperatives; *Don't…* | Lesson 10 | Lesson 10 (*Make sure you… / Don't forget to…*); Lesson 11 (packing) |
| *-ed / -ing* adjectives; *I thought it was…* | Lesson 11 | Lesson 1 (*How was it? It was exciting*); Lesson 9 (opinions) |
| *something / anything / someone / anyone…* | Lesson 12 | Lesson 12 (*Never give your PIN to anyone*) |
| *hope to / plan to / want to*; a 1–2 minute talk | Lesson 13 | Lesson 11 (verb + infinitive list); Lesson 13 (a 2-minute talk) |

**Items Level 3 passed to Level 4** (from its "Deliberately NOT in Level 3" list): present perfect → **Lessons 1–4**; second conditional → **Lesson 6**; *too / enough* (Level 3 used *too small* only as a fixed phrase) → **Lesson 7**; *might / may* (Level 3 used *maybe, probably*) → **Lesson 8**; indirect questions → **Lesson 8**; phrasal verbs as a grammar point → **Lesson 10**; gerund vs. infinitive → **Lesson 11**; relative clauses → **Lesson 12**.

**Planned overlap (on purpose):** a few earlier words come back with new grammar: *cough, dentist, appointment* (Level 2 Lesson 10 → Lesson 4), *insurance card* (Level 3 Lesson 4 → Lesson 4), *shift* (Level 3 Lesson 9 → Lesson 5), *lock, smoke alarm* (Level 3 Lesson 12 → Lesson 10), *turn on / turn off, plug in* (Level 3 Lesson 10 → Lesson 10, now as phrasal verbs), *pick up, fill out* (Level 2 → Lessons 2 and 10), *password* (Level 3 Lesson 10 → Lesson 12). They are **not** on the Level 4 picture pages. Otherwise, Level 4 vocabulary is chosen not to repeat the Level 1–3 picture pages.

### 3.3 Changes from the five-level outline (moved, merged, or limited)

Every exit can-do, core grammar strand, and life-skill theme in the Level 4 outline (`Five_Level_Map.md`) is assigned to a lesson (see 5.1). **Nothing was dropped.** These items were moved, merged, or limited, and why:

| Outline item | What this plan does | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *could* (three meanings in the outline and strand table) | Taught in three places, each with a label: **suggestion** (Lesson 4, *You could try…*), **possibility** (Lesson 8, *It could open next week*), **past ability** (Lesson 13, *I couldn't… / I was able to…*) | One word, three jobs; volunteers explain one job at a time |
| *must / mustn't* (strand table, Level 4 = P) | Placed in Lesson 7 with workplace rules and dress codes | Not in the outline's core list, but it fits workplace rules and contrasts with *don't have to* (Level 2) |
| *could / was able to* for past ability (strand table, Level 4 = P) | Placed in Lesson 13 (life stories) | What you couldn't do before and can do now is the heart of the graduation talk |
| *as … as / not as … as* (strand table, Level 4 = P) | Placed in Lesson 9 (opinions) as practice of comparatives | Comparing two plans is how people support an opinion |
| "Give, ask for, and politely disagree with an opinion" | Taught as a functional-language box in Lesson 9, not a grammar strand | There is no single structure for it; the box gives fixed phrases |
| Health history, insurance basics, dental and preventive care | All in Lesson 4; insurance is limited to reading an insurance card and the word *copay* | One health lesson keeps the topic practical and calm; insurance claims are a Level 5 theme |
| "Ask for time off and plan a trip," "leave a detailed voicemail," travel and packing | Merged into Lesson 11 | One real situation (time off to visit family) needs all three |
| Local news and community meetings | Split: news in Lesson 8, a community meeting in Lesson 9 | News pairs with *might*; meetings pair with opinions |
| Workplace theme (performance, scheduling, time off, dress codes) | Spread over Lessons 3 (checking tasks, a check-in), 5 (switching a shift), 7 (dress codes, being on time), 11 (time off) | Workplace English comes back four times; "performance" is a short, positive check-in, not a formal review |
| Housing theme (locks, home safety, utilities, saving energy) | Lesson 10, plus neighbors and a spare key in Lesson 5 and a rental application in Lesson 2 | Home repairs were Level 3 Lesson 12; Level 4 moves to prevention and bills |
| Phrasal verbs | Only **separable** verbs with an object are taught (8 verbs); others (*get on, look for*) appear as vocabulary only | Separable verbs have one clear rule (*turn it off*) that volunteers can explain |
| Defining relative clauses | Subject clauses only (*a man who called, a text that asks…*); *the man (that) I met* is recognize only | Keeps the box to one pattern; Level 5 adds more |
| Indirect questions | A first look in Lesson 8: *Do you know where / when / what time / why…?* + statement order | The outline calls it a first look; *if / whether* is Level 5 |
| Time clauses | Lesson 13, including future time clauses (*After I finish this class, I'll…*) | Links to the first conditional rule (no *will* after *if / when*) |

## 4. Course Design

### 4.1 Weekly routine (same shape as Level 3, with longer Talk Time)

Learners coming from Level 3 will recognize every step. Level 4 changes four things: the audio is **longer and closer to natural speech** (some reductions), **Talk Time gets 3 more minutes** (15), the **Read & Write block runs 7:15–7:30** every week, and the **exit ticket is a 3-sentence answer with a reason**, written in the last 5 minutes of that block.

| Clock time | Step | About | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6:00–6:08 | 1. **Opening Talk + Homework Check** | 8 min | Same partner for both. A question card on last week's grammar; partners answer in **3 sentences** and ask one follow-up question. Then compare homework. The teacher corrects only 2 items on the board. Late arrivals join a pair. |
| 6:08–6:15 | 2. **New Words** | 7 min | Picture page: point, say, repeat; a quick pair game. Learners add any word they want to their **personal word list** (the *My language* column; optional). |
| 6:15–6:30 | 3. **Listening + "Hear it, say it"** | 15 min | Before you listen → **Play 1:** Task A (main idea) → press play again → **Play 2:** Tasks B and C (details, grammar fill-in) → "Hear it": 2 reduced forms from the audio (*gonna, wanna*) → repeat 2 lines. A third play is OK if the class needs it. |
| 6:30–6:45 | 4. **Grammar Q&A + pair check** | 15 min | The two-sided question/answer box; rule of thumb; Questions and answers; Common mistakes; optional contrast tip; then 3–5 minutes of pair practice. |
| 6:45–7:00 | **BREAK** | 15 min | |
| 7:00–7:15 | 5. **Talk Time** | 15 min | The main pair or group activity of the night, usually in two rounds. Every answer needs a reason; every partner asks a follow-up. |
| 7:15–7:25 | 6. **Read & Write** | 10 min | A real-world text (150–250 words) with 4–5 questions, then a writing task (an email of 5–8 sentences, a form, or an opinion paragraph). Learners start the writing in class and finish it as homework. |
| 7:25–7:30 | 7. **Exit Ticket + Homework** | 5 min | **Never skip.** A 3-sentence answer to one question, with today's grammar and a reason. Do homework item 1 together. |

> **Timing is loose on purpose.** If the listening or Talk Time runs long, take the time from Read & Write. If you're running behind, skip in this order: (1) the Read & Write writing task (it becomes homework); (2) the pair check at the end of Grammar; (3) the second round of Talk Time. **Never skip** the listening tasks, the grammar box and mistakes table, and the exit ticket.

### 4.2 Group sizes: every activity has a large-group and a small-group version

Classes start with about 15 learners and often drop to 7–8. Each lesson's Talk Time in Section 6 lists a **15**, a **7–8**, and a **5 or fewer** version. The other steps work like this every week:

| Step | 15 learners | 7–8 learners | 5 or fewer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening Talk | Pairs (one group of 3 if odd) | Pairs | Pairs, or one group with the teacher |
| New Words game | Pairs, then 3 teams for a quick team round | Pairs, then 2 teams | One team against the clock |
| Listening tasks | Individually, then check in pairs | Same | Check together as a group |
| Grammar pair check | Pairs; swap partners once | Pairs | Pairs; the teacher joins one |
| Read & Write | Pairs read and answer; individual writing | Same | Read aloud together; individual writing |
| Exit ticket | Individual, on paper | Same | Same, or spoken to the teacher and then written |

### 4.3 Language support: tapering from Level 3 (proposed)

Level 3 has no Spanish line under instructions, a task-words page with *Español* and *My language* columns, a *My language* column on the New Words page, and on each Grammar page one short Spanish note and one tip for speakers of other languages. Level 4 learners know the routine well, so support moves from translation to **contrast**: noticing where English works differently from the learner's own language.

| Support | Level 4 proposal | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Instruction icons** | **None** (decided for all levels) | Instructions are short, plain English with the same task words every week |
| **Spanish line under each instruction** | **None** (as in Levels 2 and 3) | Learners know the task words |
| **"My language" line under each instruction** | **None** | Not needed at this level |
| **Task-words page** | Optional reference page at the **back** of the workbook (Open Question 1) | Learners new to the program can still use it; others skip it |
| **Personal word list** | **Keep** the *My language* column on the New Words page (optional; filled in by the learner) | A personal bilingual list stays useful for new, less concrete words |
| **Spanish note + tip for other languages** | **Replaced** by one optional **"Contrast tip"** box per Grammar page, written for any language, with Spanish as one example | Fair to every learner; still catches the most common Spanish-transfer errors |
| **Teacher pages** | English only, as always | Teachers need no Spanish |

> **Teacher-friendly:** volunteers never read, translate, or explain the contrast tip aloud; it is for learners to read on their own. When a learner is stuck: show it (picture, gesture, board timeline), give one more English example, point to the grammar box, let a classmate help for a few seconds, then back to English.

### 4.4 Grammar format: two-sided question-and-answer boxes

Same format as Levels 1–3: the question side on the left, the answer side on the right, one word from each column. Each box has a one-line **rule of thumb** in plain words, a short "Questions and answers" section, a "Common mistakes" table (✗ → ✓), and an optional contrast tip (student pages only). Level 4 boxes have up to 6 rows, and the present perfect lessons add a **timeline** the teacher draws on the board (a line from "my life" to "now," with X's for experiences). Example (Lesson 2):

<!-- subst -->
| QUESTION | < | < | ‖ | ANSWER | < | < |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| How long **have** | you / they | lived here? | ‖ | I**'ve** / They**'ve** lived here | **for** | three years. / six months. |
| How long **has** | he / she | worked there? | ‖ | He**'s** / She**'s** worked there | **since** | 2022. / March. |
| **Have** | you | ever moved? | ‖ | Yes, | I | **have**. |
| ^ | ^ | ^ | ‖ | No, | I | **haven't**. |
| When **did** | you | move here? | ‖ | I **moved** here | in | 2021. / three years ago. |

**Rule of thumb (Lesson 2):** still true now → *have / has* + past participle. Finished, with a past time → simple past. *for* + a length of time; *since* + a starting point.

### 4.6 Assessment and progress checks

| Tool | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| **Placement chat** | Before Week 1 or on Week 1 night | 3–4 minutes: the entry questions in Section 2.1. Decides Level 3, 4, or 5. |
| **Baseline snapshot** | Weeks 1–3 | The same 6 prompts every time: *Have you ever…? (a place or a food) How long have you lived in this city? What have you done this week? What would you do if you had a free week? What do you think about (a class topic)? Tell me about an important day in your life.* About 5 learners per week during Talk Time. The final uses the same prompts to show growth. |
| **Can-do self-check** | Weeks 1, 8, 15 | "I can…" lines + 😀 🙂 😐, read aloud by the teacher. |
| **Exit ticket** | Every lesson | A 3-sentence answer with today's grammar and a reason. The teacher sorts them into ✓ / almost / not yet and starts the next class with 2 corrections on the board. |
| **Homework** | Every lesson | 15–20 minutes: finish the Read & Write writing, a speaking task with a family member or coworker, and one older grammar point. |
| **Writing checks** | Weeks 3, 7, 10, 12, 14 | The teacher collects one writing task and writes one strength and one fix: Lesson 3 update email (Week 3), Lesson 7 first-week tips (Week 7), **Lesson 9 opinion paragraph** (Week 10), Lesson 11 time-off email (Week 12), Lesson 13 talk script (Week 14). Learners keep them in a folder to see progress. |
| **Midterm / Final** | Weeks 8, 15 | Listening (the teacher plays the audio 2 times), grammar (circle + fix the mistake), a writing task (an email at the midterm; an opinion paragraph or email at the final), and a pair speaking interview with the same 1–3 rubric as Level 3. Section 7. |

**Progress-check calendar:** Week 1: placement check, can-do self-check, baseline (about 5 learners) · Weeks 2–3: baseline (the rest) · Week 3: writing check 1 · Week 7: writing check 2 · Week 8: midterm, self-check, one goal · Week 10: writing check 3 (opinion paragraph) · Week 12: writing check 4 (email) · Week 14: talk rehearsal with peer feedback, writing check 5 · Week 15: final (baseline prompts repeated), self-check, "Ready for Level 5" decision.

## 5. The Semester at a Glance

| Week | Class | Grammar Q&A focus | Real-life function |

| 1 | **Lesson 1 (Week 1):** Have You Ever…? | Present perfect for experiences: *Have you ever…? I've never…*; simple past for the details | Meet classmates through life experiences |

### Lesson 1 (Week 1): Have You Ever…?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | First night: getting to know classmates through life experiences (places, foods, jobs, things you've tried). Basic communication. Includes orientation, the personal word list, the first can-do self-check, and the baseline snapshot. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can ask *Have you ever…?* • I can answer *Yes, I have. / No, I've never…* • I can ask a follow-up in the past (*When did you…? How was it?*). • I can tell a classmate about one experience in 3 sentences. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **R (5 min):** simple past warm-up: *What did you do last weekend?* • **I:** present perfect for experiences: *Have you ever worked in a restaurant? → Yes, I have. / No, I haven't. / No, never.* • *I've never seen snow. She's lived in three cities.* • *have / has* + past participle; a list of 20 common participles (*been, done, eaten, had, made, seen, taken, tried, worked, lived…*) • **P:** simple past for the details: *Have you ever been to Chicago? → Yes, I have. I went there in 2022. → How was it?* • **R:** *-ed / -ing* adjectives (Level 3 Lesson 11): *It was exciting.* • **Rule of thumb:** sometime in your life, no time → present perfect. Add a time (*in 2022, last year*) → simple past. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ try (a new food) • ★ travel • ★ fly • ★ snow • ★ ocean • ★ mountain • ★ desert • ★ win a prize • ★ ride a horse • ★ run a race • go camping • see a famous person |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Find Someone Who Has… (mingle):** a sheet of 10 *Have you ever…?* prompts. When someone says *Yes*, ask two follow-ups in the past (*When? Where? How was it?*) and write their name. **Round 2, Two True, One Invented:** each person says three *I've… / I've never…* sentences; the others ask follow-ups to find the invented one. **15:** whole-room mingle, then 5 groups of 3. **7–8:** mingle in one circle, then 2 groups. **5 or fewer:** one group; each person asks every other person 2 prompts. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** two learners chat on the first night of an evening class at a community center; the teacher joins and asks them to find two things they have both done. **Speakers and voices:** Lucía, a learner (F); Ahmed, a learner (M); Ms. Grant, the teacher (F). **Target length:** about 1:45. **Tasks:** A: what have they both done? B: true / false on the details; C: write *have / has / did*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** a "Meet Our Class" page with three short learner profiles (about 180 words total: *I've lived…, I've worked…, I've never…*). **Write:** a 5-sentence introduction email to the teacher: who you are, two experiences, one thing you've never done, and one goal for this class. |
| **Exit ticket** | *Tell me about something you have done (or never done).* 3 sentences: the experience, when it happened (or why not), and how it was, with *because* or *so*. |
| **Pronunciation** | Contractions *I've, she's (= she has), haven't*; weak *have* in questions (*Have you ever…?* → "Havya ever"). |
| **Contrast tip (optional)** | Spanish has a similar form (*¿Alguna vez has trabajado…?*), but English never uses a finished time with it: *I worked there in 2020* (not *I've worked there in 2020*). What does your language do? |
| **Teacher tip** | Draw a timeline: one long line, "my life → now," with X's and no dates = present perfect. Learners choose what to share; places, foods, and jobs are always safe. Never ask how or when learners came to the U.S. |

### Lesson 2 (Week 2): How Long Have You Lived Here?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | How long you've lived, worked, or studied somewhere; the address and work history part of an application (an apartment or a job). Housing and employment forms. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can ask and answer *How long have you…?* • I can use *for* and *since*. • I can choose the present perfect or the simple past. • I can fill out the address and work history part of an application. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** *for / since*: *How long have you lived here? → I've lived here for three years. / since 2023.* • *How long has she worked at the clinic? → Since March.* • *for* + a length of time; *since* + a starting point. • **I:** present perfect vs. simple past: *I've worked at the hotel since 2022.* (still true) vs. *I worked at a bakery for two years.* (finished) • *When did you move here? → In 2021. / Three years ago.* • **R:** *used to* (Level 3 Lesson 3): *I used to live with my cousins, but I've had my own apartment since 2023.* • **Rule of thumb:** see the example box in 4.4. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ application • ★ current address • ★ previous address • ★ employer • ★ position (job title) • ★ start date • ★ end date • ★ reference (a person) • ★ for (two years) • ★ since (2020) • roommate • lease |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Timeline Interviews:** each learner draws a simple timeline with 4 points (moved to this city, started a job, met a friend, started English class). The partner asks *How long have you…? When did you…?* and writes 3 sentences, then tells a new partner (*She's lived here since…*). **Round 2, At the Leasing Office:** A is the agent with a blank form; B answers (invented details are fine); switch. **15:** pairs, then rotate once. **7–8:** pairs, then one rotation. **5 or fewer:** one circle; each person reports on the person to their left. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** a woman applies for an apartment at a leasing office; the agent asks how long she has lived at her current address, about her previous address, and how long she has worked at her job. **Speakers and voices:** Marisol, the applicant (F); Mr. Chen, the leasing agent (M). **Target length:** about 1:45. **Tasks:** A: what is she applying for? B: complete the form (years at each address, employer, start date); C: write *for / since*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** a rental application with a short instruction note (about 150 words; invented). **Write:** complete the form for an invented person from a picture profile, then write 5 sentences about how long you (or the invented person) have done three things, with one *used to*. |
| **Exit ticket** | *How long have you lived in this city (or worked at your job)?* 3 sentences: how long (*for / since*), when you started (simple past), and why, with *because*. Invented answers are OK. |
| **Pronunciation** | Weak *for* /fər/; *since* /sɪns/; linking (*lived_here, worked_at*). |
| **Contrast tip (optional)** | Spanish, French, and Portuguese use the present here: *Vivo aquí desde 2019 / hace tres años.* English uses the present perfect: *I've lived here since 2019 / for three years* (not *I live here since 2019*). |
| **Teacher tip** | On the board: *since* = a point on the line (●); *for* = a stretch of time (●——●). Learners can invent addresses and jobs. Say "this city" or "this job," never "this country." No questions about income or rent. |

### Lesson 3 (Week 3): Have You Finished Yet?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | At work: checking what is done and not done, an end-of-shift handoff, and a short, positive check-in with a supervisor about how the work is going. Employment (performance). |
| **Can-do goals** | I can say what I've already done. • I can ask *Have you… yet?* and answer *Not yet.* • I can say what I've just done. • I can answer a supervisor's check-in questions and write a short update email. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** *already / yet / just*: *Have you cleaned the break room yet? → Yes, I've already cleaned it. / No, not yet. I haven't cleaned it yet.* • *Has the truck arrived yet? → Yes, it's just arrived.* • Word place: *already* and *just* go before the past participle; *yet* goes at the end. • **P:** present perfect vs. simple past: *I've finished the orders. I finished them at 3:00.* • **R:** object pronouns *it / them* (Level 3 Lesson 2). • **Rule of thumb:** *already* = done, maybe sooner than expected; *yet* = in questions and *not* sentences; *just* = a very short time ago. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ to-do list • ★ task • ★ checklist • ★ supervisor • ★ deadline • ★ restock • ★ inventory • ★ clock in • ★ clock out • ★ break room • ★ feedback • check-in (a short meeting) |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Shift Handoff (information gap):** A's checklist shows what is done (✓); B's is blank. B asks *Have you… yet?* and ticks the list; then switch with a second checklist. **Round 2, Check-in:** groups of 3: a supervisor with 3 question cards (*What have you done this week? Have you finished the training yet? What's one thing you'd like to learn?*), a worker, and an observer who ticks *already / yet* on a card; rotate roles. **15:** pairs, then 5 groups of 3. **7–8:** pairs, then 2 groups (the group of 4 has two workers). **5 or fewer:** the teacher plays the supervisor first, then learners take turns. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** the end of the evening shift at a grocery store; the night supervisor checks with two workers what is done (shelves restocked, a delivery put away) and what isn't done yet (the cooler), then gives quick positive feedback. **Speakers and voices:** Ms. Rivera, the supervisor (F); Jamal, a worker (M); Kim, a worker (F). **Target length:** about 2:00. **Tasks:** A: is all the work finished? B: check *done / not yet* on the checklist; C: write *already / yet / just*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** an email from a supervisor with a weekly checklist and a reminder about a training deadline (about 170 words; invented). **Write:** a 5–6 sentence reply email: what you've already done, what you haven't done yet, when you'll finish it, and one question. (Writing check 1.) |
| **Exit ticket** | *What have you done this week, and what haven't you done yet?* 3 sentences with *already* and *yet*, and one reason. |
| **Pronunciation** | Hearing *'s* = *has* (*It's just arrived*); *Have you… yet?* → "Havya… yet?"; stress on *JUST* and *alREADY*. |
| **Contrast tip (optional)** | Spanish *ya* can mean *already* (*Ya terminé* = *I've already finished*) and *yet* in questions (*¿Ya terminaste?* = *Have you finished yet?*). *Todavía no* = *not yet*. In English, *yet* goes at the end. |
| **Teacher tip** | Draw a checklist on the board and tick items as you say *I've already…*. Performance talk stays positive and invented; learners never discuss real problems with real bosses. |

### Lesson 4 (Week 4): How Long Have You Had It?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | Health: a new-patient health history form, describing a mild, ongoing symptom at a clinic, dental and preventive care (checkups, cleanings, a flu shot), and reading an insurance card (member ID, copay). Practical and calm. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can describe a symptom and say how long I've had it. • I can answer health history questions (*Have you ever had…?*). • I can suggest an idea with *Why don't you…? / You could… / It's a good idea to…* • I can find key information on an insurance card and a clinic letter. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **P:** present perfect for health: *How long have you had this rash? → I've had it for three days. / since Monday.* • *Have you ever had surgery? → No, never. / Yes, I had knee surgery in 2018.* • **I:** suggestions: *My back hurts. → Why don't you call the clinic? / You could try a heating pad. / It's a good idea to get a checkup every year.* • **P:** *should* (Level 2) = stronger advice; *could* = a softer idea. • **Rule of thumb:** after *could, why don't you,* and *it's a good idea to*, use the base verb (*call, try, get*). |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ checkup • ★ health history • ★ symptom • ★ allergy • ★ blood pressure • ★ rash • ★ dizzy • ★ teeth cleaning • ★ cavity • ★ flu shot • ★ prescription • copay |

| **Listening** | **Situation:** a patient checks in at a clinic: the medical assistant asks how long she has had back pain, about allergies, and what she has taken; at checkout the receptionist reminds her about a dental cleaning and her copay. **Speakers and voices:** Amina, the patient (F); Daniel, the medical assistant (M); Ms. Park, the receptionist (F). **Target length:** about 2:00. **Tasks:** A: why is she at the clinic? B: complete the form (symptom, since when, allergy); C: write *for / since / ever*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** a clinic "Stay healthy" reminder letter (about 180 words: yearly checkup, a dental cleaning every 6 months, bring your insurance card, copay; invented) and an invented insurance card. **Write:** a health history section for an invented patient, then a 3-sentence suggestion note to a friend. |
| **Exit ticket** | *A friend has had a backache for a week.* 3 sentences: ask how long, give a suggestion (*Why don't you… / You could…*), and give a reason. |

### Lesson 5 (Week 5): Would You Mind…?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | Asking a neighbor or coworker for a favor: getting the mail and watering the plants while you're away (a spare key, keeping the home safe), switching or covering a shift, borrowing something; saying yes or no politely. Housing (neighbors) and employment (scheduling). |

I'll do it." • **P:** *Can you…? → Could you…? → Would you mind …ing?* (more polite, for bigger favors) • **R:** *I'd like to* (Level 3 Lesson 7): *I'd like to switch my Friday shift.* • **R:** *will* for offers: *Sure, I'll do it.* • **R:** possessive pronouns: *Could I borrow yours?* • **Rule of thumb:** after *mind*, use *-ing*. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ favor • ★ borrow • ★ water the plants • ★ feed the cat • ★ get the mail • ★ spare key • ★ package • ★ switch shifts • ★ cover a shift • ★ be away • owe (someone) • thank-you note |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Favor Exchange (mingle):** each learner gets a favor card (*get my mail for a week; switch my Saturday shift; lend me a ladder*) and a "my week" card (free / busy). | **Listening** | **Situation:** two short scenes: (1) a woman asks her older neighbor to get her mail and water her plants while she's away; he agrees and asks where the spare key will be; (2) a coworker asks her to cover his Saturday shift; she says she can't and suggests another way. **Speakers and voices:** Patricia (F); Mr. Wilson, the neighbor (M, older); Omar, the coworker (M). **Target length:** about 2:00. **Tasks:** A: what favors do they ask for? | **Read & Write** | **Read:** a building notice about packages and a neighbor's note asking a favor (about 160 words; invented). **Write:** a 5–7 sentence email or note to a neighbor asking for a favor while you're away: the dates, what to do, where the key is, thanks, and an offer to help back. |
| **Exit ticket** | *Ask a classmate for a favor with* Would you mind…? 3 sentences: your question, their answer, and the reason (*because*). |

| **Contrast tip (optional)** | In many languages, a *yes* answer means "I'll help." *Would you mind…?* asks "Is it a problem for you?", so **No** means "No problem." Spanish *¿Te importaría…? No, para nada* works the same way. |
| **Teacher tip** | Practice the "no = yes" answer first with a thumbs-up: *No, not at all!* All favor cards, keys, and addresses are invented. Shift switches follow each employer's rules: add "Ask your supervisor first." |

### Lesson 6 (Week 6): What Would You Do?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | Imaginary situations and everyday choices: what you would do if you found money at work, if a cashier gave you too much change, if a coworker were always late, if you had a free week. Learning and thinking skills (problem solving, giving reasons). |
| **Can-do goals** | I can ask *What would you do if…?* • I can answer *If I…, I would…* • I can tell a real possibility (*If it rains, I'll…*) from an imaginary one (*If I had a car, I'd…*). • I can explain my choice with a reason. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** second conditional: *What would you do if you found $50 at work? → If I found $50, I'd give it to my supervisor.* • *If I had more time, I'd take a computer class.* • *Would you tell your coworker? → Yes, I would. / No, I wouldn't. I'd talk to my supervisor.* • *If I were you, I'd…* (advice; *were* for every person; *If I was* is common in speech and OK). • **P:** first vs. second conditional (Level 3 Lesson 6): *If I get the job, I'll buy a car.* (possible) vs. *If I had a car, I'd drive to work.* (imaginary) • **R:** suggestions (Lesson 4). • **Rule of thumb:** imaginary → past form after *if*, *would* in the other half. Never *would* right after *if*. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ imagine • ★ choose • ★ honest • ★ tell the truth • ★ keep (it) • ★ give back • ★ owner • ★ envelope • ★ extra money • ★ mistake • magic wand • dream job |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, What Would You Do? Cards:** groups get 8 everyday cards (*You find $50 on the floor at work. A cashier gives you too much change. Your neighbor's door is open at night. A friend asks you to tell a small lie.*). Each person says what they would do and why; the group finds the most common answer. **Round 2, Magic Wand Chain:** *If I had a magic wand, I'd…* → the next person starts with the last idea (*If I…, I'd…*). **15:** 4 groups of 3–4. **7–8:** 2 groups. **5 or fewer:** one group; the teacher reads the cards. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** a radio call-in segment, "What Would You Do?": the host reads a situation (a cashier gives you $10 too much change); two callers say what they would do and why, and one tells what she actually did once. **Speakers and voices:** Mike, the host (M); Grace, a caller (F); Teresa, a caller (F). **Target length:** about 2:00. **Tasks:** A: what is the situation? B: match caller → choice → reason; C: write *would / 'd / had / were*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** a short advice column (about 200 words): a reader asks, "My coworker takes long breaks, and I do extra work. What would you do?" The answer uses *If I were you, I'd…*. **Write:** a 5–6 sentence reply to a different letter (*If I were you, I would… because…*). |
| **Exit ticket** | *What would you do if you had a free week?* 3 sentences: an *if*-sentence, a second idea, and a reason. |
| **Pronunciation** | Hearing *I'd* /aɪd/ vs. *I* (*I'd go / I go*); *wouldn't* /ˈwʊdənt/; *What would you* → "Whadja" (understand it). |
| **Contrast tip (optional)** | Spanish works the same way: *Si tuviera tiempo, tomaría una clase* = *If I had time, I'd take a class.* English *would* never changes and never goes right after *if* (not *If I would have time*). |
| **Teacher tip** | Choices are light and everyday. Avoid cards about the police, immigration, lending money between classmates, or anything with a "right" answer the teacher grades. Any answer with a reason is a good answer. |

| **Listening test (6:30–6:45)** | 10 items: a clinic check-in (*How long have you had…?*) and a favor between neighbors (main idea, details, grammar word). Circle / true-false / write one word. The teacher plays the audio 2 times. | 10 items: a local news report, a time-off voicemail, and a scam call that the listener hangs up on. Same task types. |
| **Grammar test (7:00–7:15)** | 20 items: 15 circle the answer (*have / has* + participle, *for / since*, *did / have*, *already / yet*, *would / will*, *too / enough*, *much / many*, *mind* + *-ing*, *mustn't / don't have to*), 5 fix the mistake | 20 items: 15 circle (*might* + base verb, *where the meeting is / where is the meeting*, *turn it off / turn off it*, *to visit / for visit*, *enjoy traveling / enjoy to travel*, *who / that*, *before / after / when*, *could / was able to*), 5 fix the mistake |
| **Writing task (7:15–7:28)** | A 5–6 sentence email: an update to a supervisor (*already / yet*) **or** a favor to a neighbor (*Would you mind…?*) (choose one), with one reason | A 5–7 sentence opinion paragraph on a community topic **or** a 6–8 sentence time-off email (choose one), with reasons |
| **Speaking interview (4 min per pair)** | Part 1: 2 baseline prompts each, with one follow-up from the interviewer. Part 2: a pair task card: a clinic intake (*How long have you had…?*) **or** a *What would you do?* card, with reasons | Part 1: the same baseline prompts as Weeks 1–3, to show growth. Part 2: an opinion exchange (agree and disagree politely) **or** a safe answer to a scam call |
| **Rubric** | Level 3's 1–3 rubric (Communication / Target grammar / Pronunciation and fluency). For Level 4, a **3 in Communication** means the learner answers in about **3 connected sentences with a reason** **and** responds to the partner (a follow-up question, or polite agreement or disagreement). | Same |
| **Self-check** | Can-do self-check + one goal | Can-do self-check; "Ready for Level 5" decision |

> **Exam-night timing with 15 learners:** 8 pairs × 4 minutes ≈ 32 minutes of interviews. Start them during the review stations and continue during the grammar and writing tests. With 7–8 learners, about 16 minutes.

**Proposed "Ready for Level 5" standard (confirm):** attend at least 9 of the 13 lessons, complete the final, score 2 or higher in all rubric areas on the final speaking interview, and write an email or paragraph of at least 5 sentences that a reader can understand on the first reading. Learners who are not ready still graduate and are encouraged to repeat Level 4.

**Week 16: Graduation.** No new content. Each learner gives a **2-minute "My Story So Far" talk** prepared in Lesson 13 (reading from paper is fine; learners who need it may give a shorter talk). Certificates of completion. Optional potluck: ask about allergies, and don't require anyone to bring food. Hand out a "Keep practicing" sheet with the website, QR codes for all audio, and local community resources (adult education, library, consumer help, bill-help programs).
