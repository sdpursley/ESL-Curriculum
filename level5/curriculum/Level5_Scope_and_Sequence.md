## 1. Rationale and Research Basis

### 1.1 Why a Level 5, and who it is for

Level 4 graduates can talk about experiences (*Have you ever…? How long have you…?*), imagine a situation (*If I had…, I would…*), give an opinion and disagree politely, and write a short email. What they usually cannot do yet is talk about an action that is **still going on** (*How long have you been working here?*), put **two past times in order** (*By the time the inspector arrived, we had already painted*), say what they **wish** had happened, **report** another person's words, ask a **polite indirect question** (*Do you know if…?*), **confirm** a detail (*You sent it, didn't you?*), and **judge** whether something they read is reliable. Those are the skills adults need for an interview, a bill, a phone message, a school office, a meeting, and a short talk. Level 5 teaches them.

**Target learner:** an adult who finished Level 4 (or places at that level) and can hold a conversation, give a reason, and write a short paragraph with some help. About 95% speak Spanish; the rest speak a range of other languages. Most read and write in their first language. Some are repeating learners who want more speaking and writing practice.

### 1.2 Level estimates (our estimate, not an official alignment)

| Framework | Level 4 (existing) | Level 5 (this course) |
|---|---|---|
| **NRS** Educational Functioning Levels for ESL (U.S. Department of Education, adult education reporting) | Low Intermediate → **High Intermediate ESL** | Starts at **High Intermediate ESL**; aims to finish at **Advanced ESL** |
| **CEFR** (Council of Europe; "plus" levels from the 2020 *Companion Volume*) | A2+ → B1 | **B1 → B1+** (moving toward B2) |
| **CASAS** | Same life-skill content areas (see 1.3). CASAS tests can report learners on the NRS levels. We **do not** claim any score alignment | Same |

What these levels describe, in plain words (paraphrased):

- **NRS High Intermediate ESL:** the learner understands short new phrases on familiar topics, can take part in limited conversations, and has inconsistent control of more complex grammar.
- **NRS Advanced ESL:** the learner understands longer speech on familiar topics, can take part in conversations and group talk, and can read and write connected paragraphs, still with some errors.
- **CEFR B1:** the learner understands the main points of clear speech on familiar matters, deals with most everyday situations, connects sentences, and gives short reasons.
- **CEFR B1+:** the stronger half of B1: the learner keeps a conversation going, explains a point of view, and writes a simple connected text.

**The Level 5 exit goal is NRS Advanced ESL and CEFR B1+, applied to work, family, health messages, money papers, school, and community situations.** The can-do lists in Section 2 are written to match.

> **Note on test scores:** CASAS publishes score ranges for each NRS level, and the ranges differ by test series and year. Newer NRS descriptors are aligned with the federal English Language Proficiency Standards for adult education. If the program needs scores for reporting, use the current official tables, not this document.

### 1.4 Teaching principles (and where they come from)

| Principle | What it means in Level 5 | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| **A short summary, not one sentence** | Every Talk Time answer is about four connected sentences, or a spoken summary of about 30 seconds. The exit ticket is a short written or spoken summary. | CEFR B1+ descriptors for explaining a viewpoint and writing a simple connected text |
| **Grammar through real tasks** | Each grammar point is tied to one real job: present perfect continuous → an interview; past perfect → a project timeline; *wish* → a missed deadline; passive → a statement; reported speech → a phone message; tag questions → checking a form. | Task-based language teaching (for example, Rod Ellis, *Task-Based Language Learning and Teaching*, 2003) |
| **Explicit, easy-to-explain grammar** | Every box has a one-line rule of thumb. Volunteers never need grammar terms beyond the box. The hard pairs (*worked* vs. *been working*, *said* vs. *told*, falling vs. rising tags) get one board picture. | Focus on form within meaning-focused tasks |
| **Pictures with every word** | Every vocabulary word has a picture card, as in Levels 1–4. | Dual coding (Allan Paivio) |
| **Many meetings with each word** | Each word appears in New Words, the listening, a grammar box or Talk Time, Read & Write, the homework, and a later lesson. | Paul Nation, *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language* |
| **Listen for the main idea, then details** | The audio is longer, at natural speed, and it uses the full forms *going to* and *want to*. A text tip still says that people often say *gonna* and *wanna*. The first listen is for gist and the second for details. | Common listening-instruction practice (pre-, while-, and post-listening stages) |
| **Learners lead some of the talk** | Lessons 1–9 are teacher-led, with pair and group work. In Lessons 10–13 a learner leads one round (a timekeeper, a chair, or a question-asker). | The five-level outline: longer Talk Time; learners lead |

## 2. Entry and Exit Skills

### 2.1 Entry (who should start in Level 5)

A learner belongs in Level 5 if, in a short placement chat, they **can** do most of these:

- answer *Have you ever…?* and *How long have you lived here?* (*I've lived here for three years*)
- talk about an imaginary situation (*If I had more time, I would…*)
- disagree politely and give a reason (*I see your point, but…*)

…and **cannot yet** comfortably do most of these:

- answer *How long have you been working here?* with *I've been working…*
- report what someone said (*She told me to call back*)
- ask *Do you know if the office is open?*

Learners who cannot yet do the first list probably belong in Level 4. Learners who can do both lists can still take Level 5; this is the top level of the series.

### 2.2 Exit (what learners can do at the end)

**Talking and listening:** learners can…

- prepare for a job interview and talk about an accomplishment with an example
- talk about an action that is still going on (*How long have you been…?*)
- put two past times in order (*By the time…, I had already…*)
- talk about a wish or a regret, including one light third-conditional sentence (*If I had asked, I would have known*)
- explain a condition with *unless* and *as long as*
- say what was done, without saying who did it, when that is how a form or a statement is written (*The payment was received*)
- report what someone said, told, or asked
- ask a polite indirect question (*Do you know if…? / Do you know whether…?*)
- confirm a detail with a tag question (*You sent it, didn't you?*)
- say how sure they are (*It must be… / It might be… / It can't be…*) and name one reason a website is or is not reliable
- suggest a compromise and explain a result with *so… that* or *such… that*
- file a simple claim or write a complaint: what happened, what they want, and a polite close
- take part in a meeting: follow an agenda, ask a question, and say which proposal they support
- give a 2–3 minute talk with an introduction, two examples, and a conclusion

**Reading and writing:** learners can…

- read texts of 250–400 words (a job posting, a short project update, a notice, a statement plus a note, a phone-message log, a school newsletter, a web page, a meeting agenda, a claim letter) and find the main idea, the writer's purpose, and two details
- write a paragraph of about 6–8 sentences in the first half of the course
- write a 1–2 paragraph email or letter (a complaint, a request, or a thank-you) by the end
- write a talk outline (introduction, examples, conclusion) and speak from it

**Grammar they control (in practiced situations):** present perfect continuous, and present perfect vs. present perfect continuous for *How long*; past perfect with *when / by the time*; *wish* + past and *wish* + *had* + participle; a light third conditional; *unless, as long as, even though*; passive (present and past) with an optional *by*; reported speech with *said / told / asked*; embedded questions with *if / whether* and with *wh-*; tag questions for confirmation; gerunds as subjects; *so / such… that*; modals of deduction (*must / might / can't*); relative clauses with *which* and *where*; polite softeners (*I was wondering if…*); a light *should have*.

**Reviewed, not re-taught:** simple past; past continuous next to the past perfect; present perfect; second conditional; *too / enough*; *must / have to*; *will*; the first conditional; *who / that*; separable phrasal verbs; *Can / Could you…?*

## 3. Handoff from Level 4

### 3.1 What Level 4 teaches and Level 5 assumes

Level 5 Lesson 1 opens with a **brief** present-perfect warm-up, then moves to *have been* + *-ing*. Everything below is **not re-taught**. It appears in the 6:00 Opening Talk, in a homework item, or as support for a new point.

| Level 5 assumes learners know… | Level 4 teaches it in | Where Level 5 uses it |
|---|---|---|
| Present perfect for experiences and *for / since* | Lessons 1–2 | Lesson 1 (next to *have been working*); Lesson 13 (the talk) |
| *already / yet / just* | Lesson 3 | Lesson 2 (*had already*) |
| Suggestions; a calm health history | Lesson 4 | Lesson 6 (a clinic message, not a diagnosis) |
| *Would you mind…?*; *Can / Could you…?* | Lesson 5 | Lesson 10 (a compromise); Lesson 11 (*I was wondering if…*) |
| Second conditional | Lesson 6 | Lesson 3 (next to *wish* and one third-conditional sentence) |
| *too / enough*; *must / mustn't / don't have to* | Lesson 7 | Lesson 4 (a notice); Lesson 10 (*The week was too full*) |
| *might / may / could*; *Do you know where…?* | Lesson 8 | Lesson 8 (*Do you know if…?*); Lesson 9 (*must / might / can't*) |
| Opinions; *as… as* | Lesson 9 | Lesson 10 (a compromise); Lesson 12 (a meeting) |
| Separable phrasal verbs | Lesson 10 | Lesson 10, three review verbs only (*work out, call off, give in*) |
| Verb + *to* vs. verb + *-ing*; infinitive of purpose | Lesson 11 | Lesson 11 (a gerund as the subject: *Waiting on hold is tiring*) |
| *who / that* | Lesson 12 | Lesson 12 (*which, where*) |
| *before / after / when*; a 2-minute talk | Lesson 13 | Lesson 2 (*by the time*); Lesson 13 (a 2–3 minute talk) |

**Level 4's "deliberately not in Level 4" list lands here:** present perfect continuous → **Lesson 1**; past perfect → **Lesson 2**; *wish* and a light third conditional → **Lesson 3**; *unless / as long as* → **Lesson 4**; passive → **Lesson 5**; reported speech → **Lesson 6**; tag questions → **Lesson 7**; embedded *if / whether* → **Lesson 8**; modals of deduction → **Lesson 9**; *so / such… that* → **Lesson 10**; gerunds as subjects and polite softeners → **Lesson 11**; *which / where* → **Lesson 12**.

**Planned overlap (on purpose):** *interview, training, résumé* are new picture words (Level 4 did not teach a job interview). *appointment, clinic, confirm* come back in new jobs (a message, a form, a school call) and are **not** repeated as picture-page words if Level 4 already has that picture. Otherwise, Level 5 vocabulary is chosen not to repeat the Level 1–4 picture pages.

### 3.2 What this level does not hand off

This is the last level in the series. Skills that stay light on purpose, and why:

| Skill | What this plan does | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Full third conditional in every person and both clause orders | One pattern in Lesson 3: *If I had asked, I would have known* | Volunteers can explain one pattern. The rest of the paradigm is not needed for the exit can-do. |
| Every passive (*is being done, has been done, get*) | Present *is / are* + participle and past *was / were* + participle only | Those are the forms on bills, letters, and signs. |
| Full reported-speech backshift | *say / tell / ask*; present → past; *will* → *would*; *asked me to*; *asked if* | Enough to pass on a real message. |
| Rising tag questions as a debate tool | Taught as "I think this is true; please confirm," with falling intonation. The rising pattern is mentioned so learners can hear it. | The exit can-do is confirming, not cross-examining. |
| Labor law, tenant law, insurance law | Learners read a notice, ask a question, and write a polite letter. A teacher note says this is not legal advice. | A volunteer class cannot give legal advice. Local help lines go on the "Keep practicing" sheet. |
| Credit scores, loans, and debt counseling | Lesson 5 reads a simple statement and a three-line budget. No product is recommended. | The outline asked for budget and credit language, not financial advice. |
| Background noise in the audio | Not in the first build (Decision 4) | The class speaker and the listening check need clear speech. |

Every exit can-do, core grammar strand, and life-skill theme in the Level 5 outline (`Five_Level_Map.md`) is assigned to a lesson (see 5.1). **Nothing was dropped.** These items were moved, merged, or limited, and why:

| Outline item | What this plan does | Why |

The words in the file are *going to* and *want to*. | Same reason as Level 4: one clear job for the volunteer, and a file the checker can hear. Decision 4. |

| Gerund as subject (strand table says P under verb + *-ing*) | **Introduced** in Lesson 11. Level 4 already taught verb + *-ing* after another verb. | *Waiting is hard* is a new job for a form they know. |
| Worker and tenant rights; insurance claims | Lesson 4 reads a workplace notice and a building notice. Lesson 11 is a small insurance claim and a complaint letter. | One night for "read the rule," one night for "ask for a status." Not a law course. |

| Personal finance (budget, credit) | Lesson 5: read a statement (a charge, a payment, a credit that is money back) and write a three-line budget. | "Credit" here means a line on a statement, not a credit card offer. |
| Civic participation | Lesson 12: learners lead a short meeting with an agenda. | The outline says learners lead some Talk Time. This is that night. |
| Media and online information | Lesson 9, with *must / might / can't* | Judging a source needs the deduction modals. |
| Career advancement and interviews | Lesson 1 | The first night is a workshop, not a real interview with the teacher. |
| Workplace conflict and compromise | Lesson 10, peer to peer (a shared Saturday) | A role-play that accuses a boss is the wrong task for this room. |
| Personal achievement and community support | Lesson 13 | The graduation talk. |
| Changes over time | Lesson 2 (a shop getting ready to open) | Past perfect needs two past times. A renovation timeline is safer than a life story. Life stories stay in Lesson 13, and they are the learner's choice. |

## 4. Course Design

### 4.1 Weekly routine

Learners coming from Level 4 will recognize every step. Level 5 changes four things: the listening block is **20 minutes** (the file is longer), **Talk Time is 18 minutes**, **Read & Write is 7 minutes** because the text is longer and the writing is finished at home, and the **exit ticket is a short summary** (about four sentences, or 30 seconds spoken and then written).

| Clock time | Step | About | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6:00–6:06 | 1. **Opening Talk + Homework Check** | 6 min | Same partner for both. One question on last week's grammar. The answer is a short summary. Then compare one homework item. The teacher writes 2 corrections on the board. |
| 6:06–6:12 | 2. **New Words** | 6 min | Picture page: point, say, repeat; a quick pair game. |
| 6:12–6:32 | 3. **Listening + "Hear it, say it"** | 20 min | Before you listen → **Play 1:** Task A → press play again → **Play 2:** Tasks B and C → the *gonna / wanna* recognition tip → repeat 2 lines. A third play is OK. |
| 6:32–6:45 | 4. **Grammar Q&A + pair check** | 13 min | The two-sided box, the rule of thumb, questions and answers, common mistakes, then a short pair check. |
| 6:45–7:00 | **BREAK** | 15 min | |
| 7:00–7:18 | 5. **Talk Time** | 18 min | The main activity, usually two rounds. In Lessons 10–13 a learner leads one round. |
| 7:18–7:25 | 6. **Read & Write** | 7 min | A text of 250–400 words, 4–5 questions, then start the writing. Finish at home. |
| 7:25–7:30 | 7. **Exit Ticket + Homework** | 5 min | **Never skip.** A short summary with today's grammar. Do homework item 1 together. |

> **Timing is loose on purpose.** If the listening or Talk Time runs long, take the time from Read & Write. If you are running behind, skip in this order: (1) the Read & Write writing task (it becomes homework); (2) the pair check at the end of Grammar; (3) the second round of Talk Time. **Never skip** the listening tasks, the grammar box and mistakes table, and the exit ticket.

### 4.2 Group sizes

Classes often start near 15 learners and drop to 7–8. Each lesson's Talk Time in Section 6 lists a **15**, a **7–8**, and a **5 or fewer** version. The other steps work like this every week:

| Step | 15 learners | 7–8 learners | 5 or fewer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening Talk | Pairs (one group of 3 if odd) | Pairs | Pairs, or one group with the teacher |
| New Words game | Pairs, then 3 teams | Pairs, then 2 teams | One team against the clock |
| Listening tasks | Individually, then pairs | Same | The whole group checks together |
| Grammar pair check | Pairs; swap once if time | Pairs | Pairs; the teacher joins one |
| Read & Write | Pairs read; individual writing | Same | Read aloud together; individual writing |
| Exit ticket | Individual, on paper | Same | Same, or spoken first and then written |

### 4.3 Language support

| Support | Level 5 plan | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Instruction icons** | **None** | Same decision as the other levels |
| **Spanish line under each instruction** | **None** | Learners know the task words |
| **"My language" on each New Words page** | **None** | The outline moves this to the back of the book |

### 4.4 Grammar format

Same two-sided boxes as Levels 1–4. Each lesson has a one-line **rule of thumb**, a short "Questions and answers" section, and a "Common mistakes" table (✗ → ✓). Level 5 boxes have up to 6 rows. Lessons 1 and 2 add a **timeline** the teacher draws. Example (Lesson 1):

<!-- subst -->
| QUESTION | < | < | ‖ | ANSWER | < | < |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| How long **have** | you | **been working** here? | ‖ | I**'ve been working** here | **for** | two years. |
| How long **have** | you | **worked** here? | ‖ | I**'ve worked** here | **since** | 2024. |
| **Have** | you | **finished** the training? | ‖ | Yes, | I | **have**. |
| What **did** | you | **do** in your last job? | ‖ | I **helped** customers | at | a store. |

**Rule of thumb (Lesson 1):** *been* + *-ing* stresses the activity, still going on. *have* + past participle is also correct for *How long*, and it is the form from Level 4. Both answers are accepted. A finished time (*in 2022*) still needs the simple past.

| Tool | When | What |
|---|---|---|

### Lesson 3 (Week 3): I Wish I Had Asked

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | Mild regrets about class, work, or a sign-up deadline: what you wish were true now, what you wish you had done, and one sentence about a different past. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can say *I wish I had…* for a past regret. • I can say *I wish I knew…* for something I want now. • I can say one *If I had…, I would have…* sentence. • I can say what I will do next time. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** *I wish I knew the answer* (want it now; past verb). • *I wish I had asked the teacher* (past regret; *had* + participle). • **I (light):** *If I had asked, I would have known.* • **P:** *I should have asked earlier.* One row, same meaning as the wish. • **P:** second conditional for next time: *If I had another chance, I would ask on the first day.* • **Rule of thumb:** *wish* about now → past verb (*knew, had, were*). *wish* about the past → *had* + participle. | **Can-do goals** | I can say how sure I am with *must*, *might*, and *can't*. • I can name two clues (author, date, ad, a mismatch). • I can say what I would do before I believed the page. • I can tell a classmate, with a reason. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** *It must be an ad. It has a "Buy now" button.* (you are almost sure) • *It might be old. The date says 2019.* • *It can't be the city's page. The logo is different.* • **P:** *might* for a guess you are less sure about (Level 4). *must* and *can't* are stronger. • **Rule of thumb:** *must / might / can't* + *be* or another base verb. *Can't* here means "that is not possible," not "is not able." |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ website • ★ source • ★ author • ★ advertisement • ★ screenshot • ★ fact • ★ rumor • ★ date stamp • ★ logo • ★ comment • reliable • share button |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Three Pages:** groups get three invented screenshots (a city bus notice, a "amazing deal" page with no author, a neighborhood post with no date). They label each *must be / might be / can't be* and give a reason. **Round 2, Before You Share:** each person says what they would check before they forwarded the page. **15:** 5 groups of 3. **7–8:** 2 groups. **5 or fewer:** one group, all three pages. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** Noah shows Aisha a post that says their grocery store is closing. They look for the author and the date. Aisha thinks it must be a rumor. Noah finds the store's own page, which says the store is open. **Speakers:** Noah (M); Aisha (F). **Length:** 1:50. **Tasks:** A: is the store closing? B: list the clues they use; C: write *must*, *might*, or *can't*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** two short pages about the same library event (about 360 words together). One is the library's page. One is a repost with a wrong time. **Write:** a 6–8 sentence message to a friend: which page you trust, two clues, and what the person should do. |
| **Exit ticket** | *How can you tell that a page might be wrong?* Four sentences with *must*, *might*, or *can't*, and one reason. |
| **Pronunciation** | Stress the modal: *It MUST be an ad. It CAN'T be official.* *Might* stays softer. |
| **Common mix-up** | *It must an ad* → *It must be an ad*. *It can be not the city page* → *It can't be the city page*. *Must to be* → *must be*. |
| **Teacher tip** | Use only the invented pages in the lesson. If a learner brings up a real post, say: "Let's stay with our class pages." No elections, no health cures, no crime stories. "Reliable" means you can name the author and the date, and the page matches a second source. It does not mean "I like it." |

### Lesson 10 (Week 11): Can We Meet in the Middle?

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | Two coworkers share a Saturday job. They disagree, then meet in the middle. A learner keeps the time in round 2. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can say what I need and ask what the other person needs. • I can offer a middle idea. • I can explain a result with *so… that* or *such… that*. • I can use *work out*, *call off*, or *give in* in a real sentence. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I (phrases):** *What if we split the day? Let's meet in the middle. I can take the morning if you take the afternoon.* • **I:** *The week was so busy that we split Saturday.* • *It was such a long shift that we shared it.* • *so* + adjective; *such* + *a / an* + adjective + noun. • **P:** *work it out, call it off, give in*. Noun in the middle or after; *it* only in the middle (*work it out*), the Level 4 rule, reviewed not re-taught. • **R:** *too* (*The week is too full for one person*). • **Rule of thumb:** say the other person's idea in one sentence before you offer yours. *So* and *such* both mean the result was big enough to cause the next clause. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ compromise • ★ offer • ★ counteroffer • ★ priority • ★ shared task • ★ solution • ★ fairness • ★ sticky note • ★ break time • ★ swap • whiteboard • middle |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Saturday Split:** pairs get different constraint cards (morning class, a family visit, a second job). They must leave with a written compromise. **Round 2, Learner Timekeeper:** groups of 3. One learner keeps a 4-minute timer, makes sure both people speak, and reads the compromise aloud. Rotate the leader. **15:** pairs, then groups of 3. **7–8:** pairs, then one group of 3 and pairs. **5 or fewer:** one group; rotate the timekeeper. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** Camila and Jon both think they need Saturday off. Their lead, Ms. Nguyen, asks them to work it out before she calls the shift off. They split the day. **Speakers:** Camila (F); Jon (M); Ms. Nguyen (F). **Length:** 2:11. **Tasks:** A: what is the problem? B: write each person's limit and the compromise; C: write *so* or *such*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** a half-page team note about shared weekend duties (about 300 words). **Write:** a 6–8 sentence message to a coworker proposing a compromise, with one *so… that* or *such… that*. |
| **Exit ticket** | *Describe a small compromise.* Four sentences: what each person needed, the middle idea, a *so / such* result, and why it was fair. |
| **Pronunciation** | Friendly stress on the offer: *What if WE split it?* Falling tone on *Let's meet in the middle*, so it sounds like a suggestion. |
| **Common mix-up** | *The week was so busy so we split it* → *so busy that we split it*. *It was so long shift* → *such a long shift*. *Give in the argument* is the wrong pattern; *give in* has no object here. |
| **Teacher tip** | The conflict is a schedule between coworkers, not a complaint against a boss and not a pay dispute. Ms. Nguyen only asks them to decide. Do not role-play yelling. Praise the sentence that repeats the other person's need. |

### Lesson 11 (Week 12): I'd Like to File a Claim

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | Ask about a small insurance claim (a cracked phone screen, or a water stain that is already dry) and write a two-paragraph letter: what happened, what you want, and a polite close. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can start a sentence with a gerund (*Waiting on hold is tiring*). • I can soften a request with *I was wondering if…*. • I can say what was sent or attached, in the passive. • I can write a 1–2 paragraph letter. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** *Waiting on hold is tiring. Filing a claim takes time. Sending a photo helps.* The *-ing* word is the subject. • **P:** *I was wondering if you could check the claim.* • *Would you mind looking up the reference number?* (Level 4). • **P:** *The photo was attached. The form was received.* • **R:** *You have to include the policy number.* • **Rule of thumb:** a gerund subject is one activity, treated as it. The verb after it is singular: *Waiting is*, not *Waiting are*. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ claim form • ★ policy number • ★ photo • ★ estimate • ★ complaint • ★ attachment • ★ reference number • ★ customer service • ★ on hold • ★ resolution • cracked screen • water stain |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, The Call:** A is the customer with a claim card. B is customer service with a status card (*received, missing the photo, in review*). A must use *I was wondering if* and one gerund subject. Switch. **Round 2, Letter Outline:** pairs list the two paragraphs (what happened; what you are asking) before anyone writes full sentences. **15:** pairs, then switch roles with a new card. **7–8:** pairs. **5 or fewer:** one call the group coaches, then individual outlines. |
| **Listening** | **Situation:** Hector calls about a cracked-screen claim. Ms. Lane says the form was received and asks him to send the estimate. He stays polite. The phone call is the whole file. **Speakers:** Hector (M); Ms. Lane (F). **Length:** 2:02. **Tasks:** A: what is the claim about? B: complete the status and what he still must send; C: write the gerund or *was received / was attached*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** a claim-status letter from an invented insurer (about 380 words). **Write:** a two-paragraph reply (about 8–12 sentences): paragraph 1, what happened and what was sent; paragraph 2, what you are asking, with *I was wondering if* and a thank-you. (Writing check 4, collected the following week.) |
| **Exit ticket** | *Start a claim letter.* Four sentences: a gerund subject, one passive, the request, and a polite close. |
| **Pronunciation** | *I was wondering if* as one soft chunk. The gerund keeps its *-ing*: *filing*, *waiting*, *sending*. |
| **Common mix-up** | *Waiting on hold are tiring* → *is tiring*. *I was wondering if could you check* → *if you could check* (statement order). *The photo was attach* → *was attached*. |
| **Teacher tip** | The damage is a phone screen or a dry stain. Nobody is hurt, and the home is safe. Do not invent a fire, a flood, or an injury. Say: "This letter is practice. ### Lesson 12 (Week 13): The Room Where We Meet

| Part | Plan |
|---|---|
| **Theme and life skill** | A library meeting about evening hours. Learners follow an agenda, refer to a proposal, and one learner chairs a four-minute round. A second card set uses the same English for a school conference. |
| **Can-do goals** | I can point to a thing with *which* and a place with *where*. • I can still use *who* for a person. • I can ask a question from the agenda. • I can give my opinion with a reason and listen to one other opinion. |
| **Grammar Q&A focus** | **I:** *The proposal which the chair read is on page 2.* • *This is the room where we meet.* • *The person who takes notes is the secretary.* • **R:** *who / that* from Level 4. *Which* is for things. *Where* is for places. • **P:** opinion phrases from Level 4: *I think… / I see your point, but…* • **Rule of thumb:** *which* and *who* tell you which thing or person. Do not add *it* or *he* after them (*the proposal which the chair read*, not *which the chair read it*). *Where* replaces *there*. |
| **Vocabulary (12)** | ★ agenda • ★ chairperson • ★ minutes • ★ microphone • ★ handout • ★ proposal • ★ sign-in sheet • ★ name tent • ★ timer • ★ question card • library • auditorium |
| **Talk Time** | **Round 1, Read the Agenda:** groups of 4 get a one-page agenda (welcome, the proposal, questions, a decision). The group writes one sentence: the decision and one reason. | **Listening** | **Situation:** a short library meeting. Ms. Adeyemi chairs. Tom wants later hours. Grace wants the current hours, because the bus she takes stops early. They agree to a one-month try. **Speakers:** Ms. Adeyemi (F); Tom (M); Grace (F). **Length:** 2:17. **Tasks:** A: what are they deciding? B: match person → idea → reason; C: write *which* or *where*. |
| **Read & Write** | **Read:** the meeting agenda plus a 250-word proposal (about 400 words together). **Write:** a 6–8 sentence comment you would hand to the chair: what you support, one reason, and a question with *which* or *where*. |

### Lesson 13 (Week 14): A Short Talk

## 7. Midterm, Final, and Graduation

Same evening shape as Level 4. Level 5 changes: the listening is longer and is still played **2 times**, the writing is a **paragraph** at the midterm and a **short letter** at the final, and the speaking interview includes a **summary of about four sentences** and a **response to the partner**.

| | **Week 8: Midterm** | **Week 15: Final** |
|---|---|---|
| **Covers** | Lessons 1–7: present perfect continuous; past perfect; *wish* and one third-conditional sentence; *unless / as long as / even though*; present and past passive; *said / told / asked*; tag questions | All lessons, with more items from 8–13: embedded *if / whether*; *must / might / can't*; *so / such… that*; gerund subjects; *I was wondering if*; *which / where*; the talk frame |
| **Review stations (6:00–6:25)** | Grammar sort (which time is earlier?), message relay, tag-question correction, a one-minute interview | Source check (*must / might / can't*), a four-minute meeting, letter outlines, talk rehearsal |
| **Listening (6:25–6:45)** | 10 items from a practice interview and a clinic message. Main idea, details, one grammar word. The teacher plays the audio 2 times. | 10 items from a website conversation, a claim call, and a short meeting. Same task types. |
| **Grammar (7:00–7:12)** | 16 items: circle (*been working / worked*, *had / did*, *wish I had / wish I would*, *unless / if*, *was added / added*, *told me / said me*, the tag) and 4 fix-the-mistake items | 16 items: circle (*if the office is / if is the office*, *must be / must*, *so… that / so… so*, *Waiting is / Waiting are*, *which / where / who*, statement order after *wondering if*) and 4 fix-the-mistake items |
| **Writing (7:12–7:25)** | A paragraph of 6–8 sentences: a project in order, or a mild regret and a next step | A short letter of 8–12 sentences: a claim, a request to an office, or a thank-you to a mentor |
| **Speaking (about 4 minutes a pair)** | Two baseline prompts, then a pair task: a practice interview or a message relay | The same baseline prompts, then a pair task: judge a page, or agree on a compromise |
| **Score** | The Level 4 1–3 rubric (communication, the target grammar, pronunciation and fluency). A **3 in communication** means about **four connected sentences**, a reason, and a response to the partner. | Same |
| **Self-check** | Can-do lines and one goal | Can-do lines and the completion note below |

> **Exam night with 15 learners:** interviews start during the review stations and continue during the written parts. With 7–8 learners the interviews take about half as long.

**Completion note (for the teacher's folder, not a gate the learner must pass to attend graduation):** the learner came to at least 9 of the 13 lessons, took the final, scored 2 or higher on each part of the speaking rubric, and wrote a letter or paragraph of at least 8 sentences that a reader can understand on the first reading. Learners who do not meet the note still give their talk and receive a certificate. There is no Level 6 to place them into. The teacher can invite a learner to repeat Level 5.

**Week 16: Graduation.** No new content. Each learner gives the **2–3 minute talk** from Lesson 13. Reading from cards is fine. A shorter talk is fine. Certificates. An optional shared snack: ask about allergies, and do not require anyone to bring food. Hand out a "Keep practicing" sheet: the class website, how to find the audio when it is published, the public library, and a local help line for work, housing, and consumer questions. The teacher checks those local numbers before the night. The sheet does not give legal, medical, or financial advice.

## 8. Decisions

These are the choices the lesson build should follow. They are not a claim that the owner has already approved them. Change them before the lessons are published if a row is wrong.

1. **English-only student pages.** No contrast-tip box. The common mix-up is a row in the mistakes table, in English. An optional glossary sits at the back of the book, with a blank *My language* column the learner fills in. The weekly New Words page has no *My language* column.
2. **One main new point a week.** *Should have* stays inside Lesson 3. *So / such… that* stays inside Lesson 10. Phrasal verbs in Lesson 10 are a review of three verbs, not a new lesson.
3. **Not advice.** Lesson 4 is not legal advice. Lesson 5 is not financial advice, and "credit" means a credit on a statement. Lesson 6 is not medical advice. Lesson 11 is not insurance advice. The teacher script says so in one sentence, and the student page does not.
4. **Audio, when it is recorded later.** About **2:05–2:40**, **2–3 speakers in a scene**, a fourth speaker only in a separate scene, **two class plays**, full forms *going to* and *want to*, and the same recognition tip for *gonna* and *wanna* as Level 4. **No background sound** in the first build. Do not start that recording from this document.
5. **Slides.** Build them from the lesson pages, not from this scope. Lesson 1 starts from the Level 4 Lesson 13 deck. Do not invent a slide line the lesson page does not have.
6. **Sensitive prompts.** No questions about how a learner came to this country, family separation, income, debt, real diagnoses, or real employers. Practice interviews, clinic messages, statements, and claims use invented facts.
7. **Names and numbers.** All invented. Prefer names and figures the later listening check can hear. Avoid a second meaning that depends on *gonna*, *wanna*, or a string of digits the checker has failed on before.
8. **Graduation.** Everyone who wants to speak can speak. The completion note in Section 7 is for the teacher's folder. It is not a rule that keeps someone out of the ceremony.
